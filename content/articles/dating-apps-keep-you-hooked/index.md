There was a period when Josh Popkin was going on a date every night.

Sometimes two dates in one night. One night, three.

At the time, Popkin says, the volume could look like progress. He was meeting people. He was putting himself out there. He had options. But eventually he began to see something else happening: The more options he had, the harder it became to actually pick one person.

## When Does Something Helpful Become Destructive?

Dating apps solve a real problem. They put people who want to meet other people in the same place, and they make introductions possible that might never happen otherwise.

A Pew Research Center survey of 6,034 American adults, conducted in July 2022, found that three in 10 U.S. adults had used a dating site or app. Among adults under 30, 53 percent had used one.

These apps also produce relationships. Pew found that one in 10 partnered adults had met their current partner through a dating site or app. Among partnered adults under 30, that figure was 20 percent.

Users themselves were fairly divided about the experience. Pew found that 53 percent described their experience with online dating as positive, while 46 percent described it as negative.

That tension makes sense to Popkin. He does not think dating apps are inherently bad. He has come to think they can easily go from helpful to destructive.

The difficult part, as he sees it, is noticing when the thing helping someone meet people has started changing the way that person evaluates them.

## What Happens When There Is Always Another Option?

The central feature of a dating app is choice.

There is another profile. Then another. Then another. Even after a good conversation or a promising date, the larger system remains available. A user can keep looking.

Popkin says that changed something in his own behavior.

When he was going on dates every night, and sometimes more than once in a night, he was technically doing what dating apps make possible at their most efficient. He had turned access into volume.

But Popkin came to think that volume and commitment could move in different directions. The more people he could potentially meet, he says, the easier it became to remain in the state of evaluating potential people.

A 2020 study by Tila Pronk and Jaap Denissen, published in Social Psychological and Personality Science, examined what happened as people evaluated a series of potential partners online. The researchers found that participants became more likely to reject potential partners as they continued.

On average, the chance that a participant would accept a partner option fell by 27 percent from the first option shown to the last. The researchers called the pattern a "rejection mind-set."

Popkin says he recognizes that pattern.

The issue, as he came to see it, was not that each new person was worse. The issue was that another option was always waiting.

## Why Can Dating Apps Become So Exhausting?

What can feel like abundance also requires constant work.

A 2024 Forbes Health survey, conducted by the market-research firm OnePoll among 1,000 Americans who had used a dating app in the previous year, found that respondents spent nearly 51 minutes a day on dating apps.

Nearly half said they were seeking a long-term relationship. Yet 78 percent said they felt emotionally, mentally or physically exhausted by dating apps sometimes, often or always.

The figure was 80 percent among millennials, 79 percent among Gen Z, 78 percent among Gen X and 70 percent among baby boomers.

Judy Ho, a clinical and forensic neuropsychologist on the Forbes Health advisory board, told Forbes Health that people can feel "very expendable" on apps and described an "inherent competition" underneath the experience.

Rufus Tony Spann, a certified sex therapist on the same advisory board, said people experiencing dating-app burnout can become exhausted by "constantly meeting new people, failing opportunities and lies."

## Are Dating Apps Designed to Keep People Using Them?

That question has become the subject of a legal dispute.

On Valentine's Day in 2024, six plaintiffs filed a proposed class-action lawsuit in federal court in California against Match Group, the owner of Tinder, Hinge and other dating apps, according to CBS News.

The complaint alleged that the apps lock users "into a perpetual pay-to-play loop" and "gamify romance and dating" through hidden algorithms. It also targeted Hinge's marketing tagline, "designed to be deleted," calling the slogan false advertising.

Match Group rejected those claims. The company called the lawsuit "ridiculous" and said it had "zero merit."

"Our business model is not based on advertising or engagement metrics," Match Group said. "We actively strive to get people on dates every day and off our apps."

The CBS News report did not say how the case was resolved.

## What Is the Difference Between Choosing and Picking?

For a long time, Popkin says, he treated more activity as evidence that he was getting closer to the desired outcome of finding a great partner.

More matches meant more possibilities. More dates meant more chances. If one date was good, another might be better.

The apps did what Popkin says they were built to do for him: They kept him hooked.

The problem, as he has come to see it, was not that he had too few options.

It was that having endless options made it easier not to pick one.
